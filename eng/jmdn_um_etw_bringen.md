# jmdn. um etw. bringen — to deprive sb. of sth., to cost sb. sth.

> A fixed, neutral-to-written construction. Often it reports a loss
> ("it cost him his sleep / his money / his life") rather than a
> deliberate act of robbery.

---

## Grammar

| Feature | Value |
|---|---|
| Verb type | *bringen* — mixed (irregular): *bringt – brachte – hat gebracht* |
| Structure | **jmdn.** (acc.) **um etw.** (acc.) **bringen** |
| Obligatory parts | accusative person + preposition *um* + accusative thing |
| Auxiliary | *haben* |
| Reflexive variant | *sich um etw. bringen* — to lose sth. through one's own fault |
| Passive | *Er wurde um sein Erbe gebracht.* — He was done out of his inheritance. |
| Separability | *um* is a **preposition** here, not a prefix — it never |
| | fuses with the verb (**not** *umbringen*! see the trap below) |

Word order: the person follows the verb directly, the *um*-phrase
closes the clause.

> *Der Streit hat mich um den ganzen Abend gebracht.*
> — The argument cost me the whole evening.

---

## Main meanings

### 1. To deprive sb. of sth. they had

The core sense. The subject can be a person, but just as often it is a
circumstance or event — English then reaches for *cost*, *rob of*, or
*do out of*.

> *Der Betrüger hat die alte Dame um ihre Ersparnisse gebracht.*
> — The conman did the old lady out of her savings.

> *Diese Sorgen bringen mich noch um den Schlaf.*
> — These worries will end up robbing me of my sleep.

> *Ein einziger Fehler brachte ihn um seine Karriere.*
> — A single mistake cost him his career.

### 2. To take sb.'s life (euphemistic)

> *Der Unfall hat drei Menschen ums Leben gebracht.*
> — The accident claimed three lives.

> *Er kam bei dem Brand ums Leben.* (the *kommen* partner!)
> — He lost his life in the fire.

Note the pair: *ums Leben bringen* = to take a life (transitive),
*ums Leben kommen* = to lose one's life (intransitive). This is the
single most frequent pairing in the whole family.

### 3. sich um etw. bringen — to lose sth. by one's own doing

> *Mit seiner Lüge hat er sich um jede Glaubwürdigkeit gebracht.*
> — With that lie he destroyed any credibility he had.

> *Du bringst dich mit dieser Trotzhaltung um alle Chancen.*
> — With that sulky attitude you are throwing away every chance.

### 4. Fixed hyperboles

> *Dieses Kind bringt mich noch um den Verstand!*
> — That child is going to drive me out of my mind!

> *Mit so einer Aktion bringst du dich um Kopf und Kragen.*
> — A stunt like that will cost you your neck (lit. "head and collar").

## Word breakdown and etymology

| Part | Origin | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| *bringen* | OHG *bringan*, PGmc **brenganą* | to carry, convey — English **bring** |
| *um* | PGmc **umbi*, cf. Lat. *ambi-*, Gk. *amphi-* | around → **past, away** |

The whole idiom hangs on the spatial logic of *um*. Its base meaning is
"around"; from that comes the idea of **moving past the target**, and
then of **disappearance**: what was carried *around* you never reached
you. The same semantics run through *umkommen* (to perish),
*um etwas kommen* (to lose out on sth.), and *die Zeit ist um* (time is
up).

So *jmdn. um etw. bringen* is literally "to carry sth. **past**
somebody" — English *do sb. out of sth.* is built on exactly the same
image of something being steered around its rightful owner.

## Related and cognate words

| Word | Meaning | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *um etw. kommen* | to lose out on sth. | *Ich bin um den Schlaf gekommen.* |
| *umkommen* | to perish | one word! *Bei dem Beben kamen viele um.* |
| *jmdn. umbringen* | **to kill** sb. | one word, no *um*-phrase — see below |
| *sich umbringen* | to kill oneself | *Er hat sich umgebracht.* |
| *die Zeit ist um* | time is up | the same *um* = "gone by, over" |
| *jmdn. um etw. betrügen* | to cheat sb. out of sth. | same frame, always deliberate |
| *die Bringschuld* | duty to deliver in person | legal term from the same root |

---

## Don't confuse: *um … bringen* vs *umbringen*

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *jmdn. **um** etw. **bringen*** | to deprive | *Er brachte mich um mein Geld.* |
| *jmdn. **umbringen*** (one word) | **to kill** | *Er brachte mich um.* — killed me |

The only signal is whether *um* has a noun after it: **noun after *um*
→ "deprive"; bare *um* at the end of the clause → "kill".** The stakes
are high: *Er hat mich um den Schlaf gebracht* ("robbed me of my
sleep") vs. *Er hat mich umgebracht* ("murdered me").

A second giveaway is the participle: separable-prefix *umbringen*
gives *umgebracht*, while the prepositional idiom keeps plain
*gebracht*.

## Synonyms and near neighbours

| Expression | Government | Flavour |
|---|---|---|
| *jmdn. um etw. bringen* | acc. + *um* + acc. | neutral, the default |
| *jmdn. einer Sache berauben* | acc. + **gen.** | literary, elevated 🎭 |
| *jmdm. etw. entziehen* | dat. + acc. | official: to withdraw, revoke 📰 |
| *jmdm. etw. nehmen* | dat. + acc. | plain "take away" 💬 |
| *jmdn. um etw. betrügen* | acc. + *um* + acc. | to swindle out of — deliberate |
| *jmdn. etw. kosten* | acc. + acc. | "to cost sb. sth." — focus on the price |

> *Der Skandal kostete ihn das Amt.* ≈ *Der Skandal brachte ihn um sein
> Amt.* — The scandal cost him his post.

Register note: English *deprive of* sounds more formal than the German
idiom does. The closest match in tone is usually *cost sb. sth.* or the
colloquial *do sb. out of sth.*; save *deprive* and *rob of* for the
weightier contexts, and keep *berauben* for what English marks as
literary.

## Typical contexts and collocations

- **Sleep and sanity**: *um den Schlaf*, *um die Ruhe*, *um den Verstand*
- **Money**: *um sein Vermögen*, *um die Ersparnisse*, *um jeden Cent*
- **Status**: *um sein Amt*, *um die Stelle*, *um seinen guten Ruf*
- **Opportunity**: *um jede Chance*, *um die Illusionen*, *um den Sieg*
- **Life**: *ums Leben bringen* — the standard euphemism for killing
- **Hyperbole**: *um Kopf und Kragen bringen* — to land sb. in deep trouble

The subject is very often an impersonal cause: *der Krieg*, *die
Krise*, *ein Fehler*, *die Sorgen* — which is why *cost* so often
serves as the English verb.

---

## How to remember

*um* means "around, past". Picture a waiter carrying (*bringen*) the
dish **around** (*um*) you instead of to you: you end up with nothing.
That is *jmdn. um etwas bringen* — someone's share gets carried past
them.

And keep the warning pair in your ear:

> *Der Lärm hat mich **um den Schlaf** gebracht — **umgebracht** hat er
> mich zum Glück nicht.*
> — The noise robbed me of my sleep — luckily it didn't kill me.
