# harren — to await (literary, solemn)

An old, **high-register** verb meaning “to wait, to await, to abide”.
In modern spoken German it is all but extinct — **warten** has taken
its place. But it lives on in literature, poetry, biblical language
and ceremonial turns of phrase.

## Grammar

| Feature | Value |
|----------|----------|
| Type | weak, inseparable |
| Forms | *harrt — harrte — hat geharrt* |
| Government | **genitive** (bookish) or **auf + accusative** |

## Case government

### With the genitive (especially solemn)

> **einer Sache (gen.) harren** — to await something

- *der Dinge harren, die da kommen* — to await the things that are to
  come (a set, almost biblical phrase)
- *seiner Stunde harren* — to bide one’s time, await one’s hour
- *der Antwort harren* — to await the answer

### With the preposition *auf* (a shade less archaic)

> **auf etwas (acc.) harren** — to wait for something

- *auf eine Nachricht harren* — to await word
- *auf den Frühling harren* — to await the spring

## Examples in real use

- *Wir harren der Dinge, die da kommen.*
  We await whatever may come. *(near-proverbial)*
- *Eine schwere Aufgabe harrt unserer.*
  A hard task awaits us. *(solemn)*
- *Viele Probleme harren noch einer Lösung.*
  Many problems still await a solution. *(elevated journalese)*
- *Sie harrte geduldig seiner Rückkehr.*
  She patiently awaited his return. *(literary)*

Note that English **to await** does the same register work: *await*
is to *wait for* what *harren* is to *warten*.

## Register

| Register | Where you meet it |
|---------|---------------|
| 📜 archaic | Luther’s Bible, classical literature |
| 🎭 solemn | poetry, sermons, speeches |
| 📰 elevated press | leaders, literary journalism |
| ❌ conversation | effectively never |

In ordinary speech people say **warten (auf)**:

> *Ich warte auf den Bus.* — I’m waiting for the bus. ✅
> *Ich harre des Busses.* — absurd, reads as parody. ❌
> (Compare English “I await the omnibus.”)

## Derivatives and set phrases

- **ausharren** — to hold out, endure (a much livelier verb!)
  *Sie harrten bis zum Ende aus.* — They held out to the end.
- **verharren** — to remain motionless, to linger
  *Er verharrte regungslos.* — He stood stock-still.
- **beharren auf** — to insist on something *(fully alive!)*
  *Sie beharrt auf ihrer Meinung.* — She insists on her opinion.
- **beharrlich** — persistent, dogged
- **die Harrenden** — those who wait *(literary)*

> The paradox: **harren** itself is nearly dead, while its offspring
> **ausharren**, **verharren** and **beharren** thrive in modern
> German.

## Etymology and English connections

An old Germanic verb (MHG *harren*) meaning “to wait, to linger”; its
further ancestry is disputed. The English word that matches it in
feel is **to tarry** — and for the genitive constructions, **to
abide** (*to abide the coming of the Lord*). These are parallels of
register and usage rather than certain cognates, but they are the
right mental translation: whenever *harren* appears, hear *await*,
*tarry*, or *abide*, never plain *wait*.

Because German preserved the word only in the elevated style, it
carries its ceremonious tone automatically — the same way *abide*
does in English.

## How to remember

**harren** = “waiting, with a capital W”. If you meet *harren* in a
modern text, the author is almost certainly:

1. quoting or imitating older language,
2. writing in an elevated journalistic style,
3. conveying the **weight and solemnity** of the waiting (not “I’m
   waiting for the bus” but “great tasks await us”).

For active use, take **warten**. Keep **harren** for reading — you
will need it in the classics and in any sentence where something
*harrt einer Lösung*.
