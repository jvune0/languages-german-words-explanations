# Präfixe — what German prefixes roughly mean

> A map rather than a dictionary. A prefix does not pin down a verb’s
> meaning, but it almost always tells you which way to guess: where, how
> far, to what result. The key to the whole system is a single observation:
> **what detaches stays concrete**.

## Three classes of prefix

| Class | Stress | Past participle | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inseparable (just eight) | unstressed | *betreten* | abstract, often grammatical |
| Separable (open list) | stressed | *an-ge-treten* | concrete, spatial |
| Both (*durch-, über-, um-*) | depends | either pattern | literal vs figurative |

Why the split: **a separable prefix is a former independent word** — an
adverb or a preposition. It still behaves like one: it carries stress, moves
to the end of the clause, and keeps its literal sense. An inseparable prefix
has fused with the stem and lost its stress — and with the stress went the
concrete meaning.

> *Er **tritt** die neue Stelle **an**.* — the prefix detaches and goes last.
> *Er **betritt** den Raum.* — the fused prefix stays put.

## 1. Inseparable prefixes

There are only eight of them, and the list is worth memorising: it settles
both the stress and the *ge-* question in the participle.

> **be- · ge- · er- · ver- · zer- · ent- · emp- · miss-**

| Prefix | Core sense | Examples | English kin |
|---|---|---|---|
| *be-* | make the object the target; “cover” | *betreten, bemalen* | *beset, bewitch* |
| *ge-* | “together, to a result” (fossil) | *gehören, gefallen* | *y-* in *enough* |
| *er-* | achieve a result; come into being | *erreichen, erwachen* | — |
| *ver-* | away / to the end; awry; turn into | *verbrauchen, verlaufen* | *for-* |
| *zer-* | to pieces, apart | *zerbrechen, zerstören* | *dis-* |
| *ent-* | remove, de-; sometimes onset | *entfernen, entstehen* | *un-* in *untie* |
| *emp-* | variant of *ent-* before *f* | *empfangen, empfehlen* | — |
| *miss-* | wrongly, badly | *missverstehen, misslingen* | *mis-* |

### *be-* — the transitivity prefix

Its main job is grammatical rather than semantic: it **pulls the goal into
the accusative** and makes the verb transitive.

| Without the prefix | With *be-* |
|---|---|
| *in den Raum treten* (where to?) | *den Raum betreten* (what?) |
| *auf einen Berg steigen* | *einen Berg besteigen* |
| *über etwas sprechen* | *etwas besprechen* |
| *in einem Haus wohnen* | *ein Haus bewohnen* |

Its second flavour is “cover all over”: *bemalen* (paint all over),
*bewaffnen* (arm), *bedecken* (cover). English *be-* once did exactly the
same work: *bespeak*, *beset*, *bewitch*, *besiege*, *bedeck*.

### *ver-* — the most polysemous one

| Shade | Examples |
|---|---|
| away, used up, gone for good | *verkaufen, verbrauchen, verschwinden* |
| wrongly, off course (often with *sich*) | *sich verlaufen, sich versprechen* |
| turning into a new state | *verbessern, vergrößern, verfilmen* |
| closing, joining shut | *verschließen, verbinden, verkleben* |
| spending (time) | *verbringen, verschlafen* |

The English cognate is *for-*: *forget* (“lose from the mind”), *forgive*,
*forbid*, *forlorn*, *forsake* — the same “away, astray, for good”.

### *er-* — “to the point of achieving”

From ancient *ur-* “out of” (cf. *der Ursprung*, the well-spring). Hence its
two modern senses: **carrying through to a result** (*erarbeiten* — earn by
work, *erkämpfen* — win by fighting, *erschießen* — shoot dead) and
**coming into being** (*erwachen*, *erblühen*, *erröten*).

## 2. Separable prefixes

Many of them, all stressed, and nearly all still live prepositions or
adverbs — which is why the English cognates line up so neatly.

| Prefix (English kin) | Sense | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| *ab-* (*off*) | away, down; completion | *abfahren, abschließen, abnehmen* |
| *an-* (*on*) | up against, to; switch on; onset | *ankommen, anmachen, anfangen* |
| *auf-* (*up*) | upward; open; use up | *aufstehen, aufmachen, aufessen* |
| *aus-* (*out*) | outward; to the end; switch off | *ausgehen, austrinken, ausmachen* |
| *bei-* (*by*) | alongside, in support | *beitreten, beibringen, beilegen* |
| *ein-* (*in*) | inward; falling into a state | *einsteigen, einladen, einschlafen* |
| *mit-* (*with*) | along, together | *mitkommen, mitmachen, mitteilen* |
| *nach-* (*after*) | after; again; think through | *nachlaufen, nachdenken* |
| *vor-* (*fore-*) | forward; in advance; display | *vorstellen, vorlesen* |
| *zu-* (*to*) | towards; shut; increase | *zuhören, zumachen, zunehmen* |
| *zurück-* (*back*) | back | *zurückgeben, zurücktreten* |
| *weg-* (*away*) | away | *weggehen, wegwerfen* |
| *los-* (*loose*) | let go; sudden start | *loslassen, losfahren* |
| *fort-* (*forth*) | away; onward | *fortgehen, fortsetzen* |
| *hin-* / *her-* | away from me / towards me | *hingehen* / *herkommen* |
| *zusammen-* | together | *zusammenarbeiten, zusammenbrechen* |
| *entgegen-* | towards, against | *entgegenkommen, entgegentreten* |

**The *hin-* / *her-* pair** has no English equivalent: *hin* = **from here
to there**, *her* = **from there to here**. Hence *hinausgehen* (go out of
where I am) vs *herauskommen* (come out towards me), colloquially clipped to
*rausgehen* / *rauskommen*.

## 3. The two-way prefixes: stress decides

*durch-, über-, um-, unter-, wider-, wieder-, hinter-, voll-* work both
ways. The rule holds almost without exception:

> **Stress on the prefix → literal, spatial sense, prefix separates. Stress
> on the stem → figurative sense, prefix stays fused.**

| Verb | Stress on prefix | Stress on stem |
|---|---|---|
| *übersetzen* | ferry across | translate a text |
| *umfahren* | run over, knock down | drive around |
| *durchschauen* | look through (papers) | see through someone |
| *unterstellen* | put underneath, shelter | impute, insinuate |
| *wiederholen* | fetch back | repeat |
| *umschreiben* | rewrite | paraphrase |

The participle gives the game away instantly:

> *Er hat den Fluss **überge**setzt.* — He ferried across the river.
> *Er hat das Buch **über**setzt.* — He translated the book.

Note the English mirror: *overtake*, *undertake*, *withstand* also fuse
their prefix and go figurative, while *take over*, *stand by* stay literal
and separable. Same instinct, opposite word order.

## 4. Noun and adjective prefixes

| Prefix | Sense | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| *un-* | negation | *unmöglich, das Unglück, die Unruhe* |
| *un-* | ⚠️ enormous size | *die Unsumme, die Unmenge, die Unzahl* |
| *ur-* | primal, original | *der Urwald, uralt, die Ursache* |
| *erz-* | arch-, utterly | *der Erzbischof, erzkonservativ* |
| *miss-* | mis-, failed | *das Missverständnis, der Misserfolg* |
| *ge-* | collective | *das Gebirge* (← *Berg*), *das Gerede* |

⚠️ *die Unsumme* is not a “non-sum” but a **vast sum**. Same with *Unmenge*
and *Untiefe* (which means both “shallows” and “abyss” — a genuine trap).

## Seeing it work

One stem, several prefixes — and the tables above turn out to give a
workable expectation:

| Verb | What the prefix says | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *einsteigen* | inward | get in (a vehicle) |
| *aussteigen* | outward | get out |
| *besteigen* | goal → object | climb, ascend |
| *sich versteigen* | astray | overreach oneself |
| *ersteigen* | to the result | scale, get to the top of |
| *übersteigen* | over and beyond | to exceed |

⚠️ But this is **an expectation, not a formula**. Centuries have worn the
senses down, and some verbs simply have to be learnt: *bekommen* means “to
receive”, not “to become” — the classic false friend for English speakers —
and *verstehen* means “understand”, with no trace of “away” or “astray”.

## How to tell whether a prefix separates

1. **Listen for the stress** — the primary and nearly infallible test.
2. **Check the list of eight** inseparables: *be-, ge-, er-, ver-, zer-,
   ent-, emp-, miss-*. It scans as a two-beat chant.
3. **Look at the participle**: no *ge-* means the prefix is inseparable.
4. Verbs in *-ieren* have no prefix and take no *ge-*: *studiert*,
   *telefoniert*. 📎 More in [Partizip II](Partizip_II.md).

## How to remember

One sentence covers the whole system: **what detaches stays concrete**.

- A **stressed** prefix was once a free-standing word and still acts like
  one: it moves to the end of the clause, lets *ge-* slip inside it, and
  honestly means “up”, “out”, “back”.
- An **unstressed** prefix has fused for good, never moves, admits no *ge-*
  — and its meaning is just as fused and abstract: “make it the object”
  (*be-*), “through to the result” (*er-*), “astray” (*ver-*), “to bits”
  (*zer-*).

One pair to keep for life: *Er **ist** über den Fluss **überge**setzt und
**hat** dann das Buch **über**setzt.* — “He ferried across the river, then
translated the book.” One prefix, two stresses, two grammars.
